## 2. Summary

**Problem.** The paper states no research question and no hypothesis. Its objective (Section 1 para 1; Abstract) is to present CheckIfExist, "an open-source tool for real-time validation of bibliographic references against multiple scholarly databases, designed to address the growing problem of citation hallucinations in AI-generated academic content." The problem, as the paper frames it, has four parts: LLMs generate references that are stylistically plausible and correspond to no published work (Section 1 para 3); reference managers catalogue such a reference "alongside legitimate citations without any indication of its spurious nature" (Section 1 para 4); manual verification through Web of Science, Scopus, or Google Scholar "scales poorly and interrupts the writing workflow" (Section 1 para 4); and commercial detectors meter or charge for access, which the paper treats as an equity problem for "researchers in resource-constrained environments" (Section 1 para 4). The stated target is instant, free, multi-source verification (Section 1 para 5).

**Method.** No family from the method menu applies: there is no experiment, no correlational or archival analysis, no survey, and no qualitative design. By the menu's calibration table this is a systems (tool) paper, to be held to architecture decisions, scalability evidence, real-world deployment, and engineering contribution rather than to baselines, significance, or ablations. It has no evaluation, so there is no evaluation family to name either (N1, N22). In place of a method the paper reports a design. Structurally it is four modules (Section 3 para 1) — LaTeX-command filtering on input, BibTeX parsing, a multi-source search service carrying the cascading verification logic, and a presentation and export layer — implemented as a React/TypeScript browser application. Procedurally it is a cascade over three metadata APIs: CrossRef first at three candidate rows, Semantic Scholar if CrossRef returns nothing, and, when `bestScore < 70` or an issue is flagged, a fallback that queries Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex and intersects author family names across all three sources (Algorithm 1 lines 1-32; Figure 1; Section 3 para 2). Matching is normalized Levenshtein title similarity on lowercased, alphanumeric-only strings (Eq. 1; Section 3 para 6); author similarity is the proportion of database family names contained in the query string; and a fabricated-author flag fires on capitalized query tokens matching neither title words, journal, year, nor any real family name (Section 3 para 7). Confidence comes from one of two formulas (Eqs. 2-3) adjusted by penalties of -10 to -20 and a multi-source bonus, with thresholds and penalties described as "empirically calibrated" in Footnote 2. What the choice of a design description implies: it can show that a procedure is well-formed and implementable; it cannot show that the procedure separates real references from fabricated ones. Nothing in the reported method bears on how often the tool is right in either direction.

**Results.** There is no results section, no dataset, no descriptive statistics, no measurement table, and no inferential statistics, so `references/quantitative-results.md` has no object here (N1). Four artifacts stand in the place of results: Algorithm 1 (p4), Figure 1 (p5), Eqs. 1-3 (p6), and Table 1 (p7). Table 1 is a 13-row feature checklist against Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote, and JabRef in which CheckIfExist is the only tool marked ✓ on immediate validation, hallucination detection, fake author detection, batch verification, multi-source validation, and corrected BibTeX output, and the only tool marked – on reference organization and word-processor integration; on cloud synchronization JabRef is marked – as well. The only quantities the paper attaches to its own system are a three-candidate CrossRef fetch (Algorithm 1 line 2), an 800 ms batch interval (Section 4 para 2), the thresholds 50, 70, 80, and 90, the +10 bonus, and the -10 to -20 penalties (Algorithm 1; Figure 1; Section 3 para 8). None is a measurement. Every performance statement in the paper — "achieves higher recall than any single-source approach while maintaining precision through multi-source confirmation" (Section 3 para 2), "particularly effective at detecting LLM hallucinations" (Section 3 para 7), results "within seconds" (Abstract; Section 4 para 1) — is asserted rather than shown.

**Discussion.** Section 5 is titled Conclusions and does two things. It enumerates five use cases (Section 5 para 1): authors validating reference lists before submission; researchers checking LLM-suggested citations; reviewers auditing manuscripts under review "identifying potential fabrications warranting author clarification"; publishers integrating validation into submission pipelines; and bibliometricians filtering spurious references from datasets. It then restates the contribution and the motivation (Section 5 para 2). The significance the author claims is that the tool "enables researchers to validate citations at the speed of modern content production" and "complements rather than replaces existing reference management infrastructure, filling a specific gap in the scholarly toolkit". Because there are no results, there is no interpretation of results and no account of why any result came out as it did; the practical value is the paper's principal claim and is expressed entirely as those five use cases. Stated limitations: none, anywhere in the paper — there is no limitations section and no caveat paragraph (N24). Future research and next steps: none stated (N24). The conclusion therefore summarizes the contribution and the motivation but not methods or results, and it does reiterate significance. Acknowledgements, funding, and conflict-of-interest statements: none of any kind; the paper has no such section (N23). The Availability section (p8) gives the MIT License and the hosted tool URL, but no repository, version, release date, or commit (N16).

### Importance

**Verdict.** The problem is real and the paper argues its urgency clearly, but the contribution it actually delivers is a design sketch of a database-lookup utility whose headline claim, detecting citation hallucinations, is never tested and is narrower in mechanism than in title, so a reader should take the paper as a pointer to a free tool plus a motivating argument, not as evidence that hallucinated citations can be detected this way or that the tool fills a gap other tools leave open.

**I1 — Potential design or analysis problem: the question (authenticity) and the mechanism (existence of a similar database record) diverge on the hallucination types the paper uses to motivate itself.**

- **Location**: Title; Section 1 para 3 (p2); Section 3 paras 7–8 (p6).
- **Observation**: The paper's problem is hallucinated citations, including "subtle alterations of real papers, such as expanding author initials into guessed first names or paraphrasing titles" (Section 1 para 3). The mechanism described is normalized Levenshtein title similarity with an 80% threshold and author matching by family-name containment in the query string (Section 3 paras 7–8). On that description, an entry with the right family names and guessed first names satisfies the author check; whether the fabricated-author heuristic catches the guessed first name, and how it would distinguish it from a genuine first name, is not stated, and nothing is said about paraphrased titles.
- **Evidence or criterion**: N20; Section 1 para 3; Section 3 para 7.
- **Why it matters**: The title's contribution ("Detecting Citation Hallucinations") is broader than the mechanism supports. As described, the tool answers "does a similar record exist" rather than "is this citation authentic", and the two questions part company exactly on the subtle cases the paper cites as evidence that the problem is serious.

**I2 — Demonstrated inconsistency: the big idea as sold (three-source cross-validation) is not the mechanism as specified.**

- **Location**: Section 3 para 2 (p3); Table 1 (p7); Algorithm 1 line 8 (p4); Figure 1 (p5).
- **Observation**: Multi-source validation is the paper's distinguishing idea: "maintaining precision through multi-source confirmation" (Section 3 para 2), and "Multi-source validation" is a Table 1 row only CheckIfExist ticks. In Algorithm 1 the fallback that queries Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex and intersects author lists runs only when `bestScore < 70` or an issue is detected (line 8); a CrossRef match scoring 70 or above with no issue is returned on CrossRef alone. Section 3 para 2 and Figure 1 also describe a cascade (next source only if the previous fails) while Algorithm 1 lines 10–18 query both fallback sources and take a three-way intersection.
- **Evidence or criterion**: C12; C5.
- **Why it matters**: For the common case of a good CrossRef hit the tool is a single-source check, so the claimed advance over single-database lookup applies only to the low-scoring or flagged minority, and the paper's three descriptions of that minority path do not agree with one another. The publisher use case is sold on exactly this mechanism — "the multi-source validation providing higher confidence than single-database checks" (Section 5 para 1).

*Cross-references.* That absence from the three databases is read as non-existence, which is what most limits how much of the stated contribution survives contact with an ordinary bibliography, is under Generalizability (G1). That no measurement of any kind supports the significance claim, including Footnote 2's implied calibration, is under Credibility (C1, C2). That the Abstract and Conclusions state the urgency case more strongly than the body is under Credibility (C6). ### Credibility

**Verdict.** A reader should treat this paper as a design note rather than as evidence that the tool works: it is an unreviewed arXiv preprint (v1, page-1 stamp) that reports no test of the tool on any reference, its prose, pseudocode and flowchart describe three different decision procedures so the method cannot be pinned down, and its performance claims — higher recall with maintained precision, empirically calibrated thresholds, fabricated-author detection that is "particularly effective" — are asserted without a single measurement.

*Inapplicable parts.* The qualitative trustworthiness checks and self-audit do not apply: no qualitative data was collected. The participant and demand-characteristic checks do not apply: no people took part. Of the experiment-design validity threats, only construct validity applies, because there is no experiment.

**C1 — Not reported: the tool is never evaluated.**

- **Location**: Abstract; Section 1 para 5 (p2); Section 3 para 2 (p3); Section 4 para 1 (p6); Section 5 paras 1–2 (p7–8); the paper has no evaluation or results section.
- **Observation**: No set of known-real and known-fabricated references, no precision, recall, accuracy, false-positive or false-negative rate, no example run (input to output), no user or deployment data, and no latency measurement behind "within seconds" (Abstract; Section 1 para 5; Section 4 para 1) and "instant" (Abstract; Section 5 para 2).
- **Evidence or criterion**: N1, N3, N22; the reporting red flag "missing methodological details" in `references/experiment-design.md`; the requirement for effect or performance estimates with units in `references/quantitative-results.md`.
- **Why it matters**: Every statement that the tool detects hallucinations rests on the description alone. A reader has no basis for estimating how often it flags a fabricated reference or how often it flags a real one — and since Footnote 2 implies that discrimination data were produced, the absence is a withholding of the one thing that would size the contribution.

**C2 — Not reported: the "empirical calibration" has no data, procedure, or result.**

- **Location**: Footnote 2 (p6); Section 3 para 8 (p6).
- **Observation**: "Threshold and penalty values were empirically calibrated to optimize discrimination between valid references and known hallucinations, with author-related discrepancies weighted more heavily given their stronger diagnostic signal." The calibration set (size, provenance, how hallucinations were produced and labeled, which models generated them, which fields and languages the valid references came from), the procedure, the objective, the resulting discrimination, and any evidence for the "stronger diagnostic signal" are all absent — while Section 2 para 2 itself states that hallucination rates range "from 6% to over 30% depending on model, prompting strategy, and domain".
- **Evidence or criterion**: N2, N31; Section 2 para 2; "missing methodological details" in `references/experiment-design.md`; the model-tuning and validation items in `references/quantitative-results.md`.
- **Why it matters**: The thresholds (50, 70, 80, 90) and penalties (−10 to −20) are the entire decision rule, and this footnote is the only place the paper implies any empirical work was done. As reported, the calibration is indistinguishable from thresholds chosen by hand, whatever discrimination it achieved is in-sample by construction, and a reader cannot tell whether the numbers transfer to their own field, language, or model. This is a reporting gap, not evidence that no calibration was done.

**C3 — Potential design or analysis problem: performance claims are written as achieved results, with no comparator ever instantiated.**

- **Location**: Section 3 para 2 (p3), last sentence; Section 3 para 7 (p6), last two sentences; Section 5 para 1 (p8).
- **Observation**: "By cascading through these sources and cross-validating author information, the system achieves higher recall than any single-source approach while maintaining precision through multi-source confirmation" and "This multi-source confirmation is particularly effective at detecting LLM hallucinations" are stated as outcomes. The only support is the database-coverage argument in Section 3 para 2 and a citation (Nicholas et al., 2025) for the premise that hallucinations insert fake author names, not for the effectiveness. The single-source alternative the sentence names is never run, and neither recall nor precision is measured.
- **Evidence or criterion**: N1; N29; C12; the counterfactual requirement in `references/experiment-design.md`; the claim–evidence mismatches in `references/quantitative-results.md` ("directionally correct claims that overstate magnitude or precision"; "mechanistic conclusions supported only by association").
- **Why it matters**: "Achieves" and "particularly effective" are the vocabulary of measurement and carry none. The sentence is the paper's central performance claim, and per I2 the mechanism it credits is inactive on the path most references take, so even the argument-from-coverage version of it does not follow from the design as specified.

- **Location**: Algorithm 1 lines 3–5, 6, 8, 10–11, 18, 21–23, 27, 30, 32 (p4); Figure 1 nodes "Compute Confidence Score", "Score > 70%?", "Score > 80%?", the "Semantic Scholar API → OpenAlex API" chain and the "Mismatch" arrow (p5); Section 3 para 2 (p3); Section 3 para 8, Eqs. 2–3 (p6).
- **Observation**: The fallback fires at `bestScore < 70` in Algorithm 1 but at "Score > 70%?" = No in Figure 1, which disagree at exactly 70, and Algorithm 1's thresholds are unitless while Figure 1's are percentages (C1). The thresholds are 70/50 in Algorithm 1, 70/80 in Figure 1, and 80/90 applied to component similarities in Eq. 2 (C3). ### Novelty

**Verdict.** Read this as incremental engineering: the paper assembles public metadata-API lookups, a normalized Levenshtein title match, surname containment and threshold penalties into a free browser tool, and its one distinctive idea — flagging authors not confirmed across CrossRef, Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex — is specified in two conflicting ways, never tested, and never compared with the automated bibliography checkers and commercial detectors the paper itself says exist, so the "fills this gap" claim rests on a table whose comparators are reference managers that, by the paper's own description, do not attempt verification.

**N1 — Potential design or analysis problem: the only comparison is against tools that do not attempt the task.**

- **Location**: Section 4.1, Table 1 and para 2 (p7); Section 1 para 4 (p2); Section 2 para 2 (p2–3); Abstract; Section 5 para 2 (p8).
- **Observation**: Table 1 sets CheckIfExist against Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote and JabRef, which Section 1 para 4 itself describes as "fundamentally designed for organization rather than validation". The six rows on which CheckIfExist alone scores ✓ (Immediate validation, Hallucination detection, Fake author detection, Batch verification, Multi-source validation, Corrected BibTeX output) are verification functions those tools do not attempt; on the rows they share (Bibliography formatting, BibTeX export, Open source, Unlimited free usage) CheckIfExist is not distinguished. The two tool classes the paper names as performing verification — bibliographic databases (Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar; Section 1 para 4) and "commercial services for hallucination detection" (Section 1 para 4; Abstract) — are absent from the table, as is the automated bibliography analysis of Dunford et al. (2024), which the paper cites for other purposes. No criterion behind any cell is stated, no tool version or date is given, and "Bibliography formatting ✓" for CheckIfExist rests on APA-only output (Section 4 para 3) while the compared tools format in many styles.
- **Evidence or criterion**: Table 1; Section 1 para 4; Section 4.1 para 2; N4; N5; N25; the comparator-extrapolation mismatch in `references/quantitative-results.md`; "is there a control group?" in `references/experiment-design.md`.
- **Why it matters**: "The proposed tool fills this gap" (Abstract) and "filling a specific gap in the scholarly toolkit" (Section 5 para 2) are only as strong as the comparator set. A comparison whose comparators lack the feature by design cannot fail: it shows the gap exists but says nothing about whether this tool fills it better than the alternatives the paper itself names. A practitioner's real choice is between this tool and the other verifiers, and the paper does not support that choice.

**N2 — Not reported: by the paper's own account the novelty is access, not capability, and the access comparison is unnamed.**

- **Location**: Abstract; Section 1 para 4 (p2); Section 4.1 para 2 (p7).
- **Observation**: The Abstract concedes that "Commercial hallucination detection services" are "increasingly available" and objects only to their "restrictive usage limits on free tiers" or "substantial subscription fees"; Section 4.1 para 2 states the differentiator against them as "unlimited free verification". So relative to reference managers the new thing is the verification function, and relative to the services that already verify, the new thing is the price. The services are never named and their limits and fees never quantified (N4), and the one citation attached to the freemium claim, Zhu et al. (2025), is titled "Evaluating the potential risks of employing large language models in peer review" (N29).
- **Evidence or criterion**: Abstract; Section 4.1 para 2; N4; N29.
- **Why it matters**: A reader should weigh the paper as an accessibility contribution (free, open, browser-based) rather than a capability contribution, and even that framing cannot be checked because the services being undercut are not identified.

**N3 — Not reported: no component is identified as new relative to prior work, and the closest cited predecessor is never positioned as prior art.**

- **Location**: Section 3 paras 2, 3, 6, 7, 8 (p3, p6); Eq. 1; Eq. 3; Section 1 para 3 (p2); Section 2 para 2 (p3); References, Dunford et al. (2024) (p9).
- **Observation**: The paper has no related-work section (N5) and claims no technique as new. Its elements are a textbook metric (Eq. 1, normalized Levenshtein on lowercased alphanumeric strings), lookups on three public APIs each cited to that database's own description paper (Hendricks et al. 2020; Ammar et al. 2018; Priem et al. 2022 — Section 3 para 2), family-name containment (Section 3 para 7), a mean of four similarities with fixed penalties (Eq. 3; Section 3 para 8), and a LaTeX command filter presented as "A critical preprocessing step" (Section 3 para 3) whose command list is not given (N17). The one cited work whose title describes an automated bibliography check for integrity issues — Dunford et al. (2024), "Using automated analysis of the bibliography to detect potential research integrity issues" — is cited only for "chimeric" fabrications and the cost of manual detection (Section 1 para 3; Section 2 para 2) and is never compared with the tool.
- **Evidence or criterion**: N5; N17; Section 3 paras 2, 3, 6, 7, 8; Eq. 1; Eq. 3; Section 1 para 3; Section 2 para 2; the References entry for Dunford et al. (2024).

**A1 — Potential design or analysis problem: "existence" and "hallucination" are never operationalized.**

- **Location**: Title and Abstract (p1) versus Section 3 paras 6–8 (p6).
- **Observation**: The paper claims to detect "citation hallucinations" but defines neither hallucination nor existence as a measurable variable. The construct is replaced without comment by normalized Levenshtein similarity on lowercased, alphanumeric-stripped strings (Eq. 1), family-name containment (Section 3 para 7), and a weighted composite (Eqs. 2–3), of which two of the four component similarities have no defining formula and the rule selecting between the two formulas is undefined (N7, N8).

**A2 — Potential design or analysis problem: the fabricated-author test assumes every source returns the record.**

- **Location**: Algorithm 1 lines 18–19 and 26–27 (p4); Section 3 para 2 (p3); Section 3 para 7 (p6).
- **Observation**: `confirmedAuthors` is the three-way intersection and `suspectAuthors` is the union minus that intersection, with any non-empty `suspectAuthors` raising the user-facing issue "Potential fabricated authors". Section 3 para 2 states that coverage differs by source and that "metadata quality varies", so the paper's own premise for querying three databases predicts that authors of real papers will routinely be missing from one source's record. The paper does not say whether `ssResult` and `oaResult` may be empty or hold more than one candidate (N10), nor how name-form variation is handled (N18).
- **Evidence or criterion**: Algorithm 1 lines 18–19, 26–27; Section 3 para 2; N10; N18; N1.
- **Why it matters**: The mechanism the paper advertises as "particularly effective at detecting LLM hallucinations" (Section 3 para 7) fires on partial coverage and name-format mismatch, not only on fabrication, and the paper bounds neither frequency. The premise that justifies the three-source design is the same premise that predicts the mechanism's false positives, so whether "fake author detection" is a clever signal or a false-positive generator turns on exactly this, and the paper reports nothing that would settle it.

**A3 — Potential design or analysis problem: cross-source agreement is assumed to be independent corroboration.**

- **Observation**: Confidence is raised when an author appears in CrossRef, Semantic Scholar, and OpenAlex, and the paper generalizes this to "higher confidence than single-database checks" (Section 5 para 1). Section 3 para 2 describes OpenAlex as "aggregating data from CrossRef, PubMed, ORCID, arXiv, and other repositories" — so an OpenAlex author list may be a CrossRef author list re-served, not a second observation of it.
- **Evidence or criterion**: Algorithm 1 lines 18, 21–22; Section 3 para 2; Section 5 para 1.
- **Why it matters**: Where OpenAlex's record derives from CrossRef's, the intersection adds no information, the +10 bonus is awarded for self-agreement, and a metadata error in CrossRef is "confirmed" rather than caught. - **Observation**: Section 4 para 3 states that outputs "derive from authoritative metadata retrieved from the scholarly databases rather than potentially erroneous input, ensuring correctly formatted citations with validated bibliographic details". **E1 — Not reported: the confidentiality of the bibliographies the paper invites users to submit.**

- **Location**: Section 5 para 1 (p7–8); Section 3 para 1 (p3); Availability (p8).

**E3 — Potential design or analysis problem: the tool is directed at adjudicative uses with no reported reliability.**

- **Location**: Section 5 para 1 (p7–8); Algorithm 1 line 27 (p4).
- **Observation**: The paper directs the tool at consequential, gatekeeping uses: "Reviewers can efficiently audit citations in manuscripts under review, identifying potential fabrications warranting author clarification" and "Publishers can integrate reference validation into submission pipelines". A false positive in this deployment is an unfounded fabrication allegation against an identified author inside peer review or a submission pipeline, where the author may never see the tool's reasoning, and the harms fall hardest on researchers citing under-indexed genres and bearing non-Anglophone names — exactly the "researchers in resource-constrained environments" the paper says it wants to serve (Section 1 para 4). Recommending an instrument for that role with no reported error rate places the cost of its unknown reliability on the accused.

**E4 — Not reported: no risk, failure mode, or dual-use consideration, including the one the paper's own argument implies.**

- **Location**: Whole paper (no Limitations or Future Work section); Section 2 para 3 (p3); Availability (p8); Section 4.1 para 2 (p7); Algorithm 1 line 32 (p4).

A paper that discusses adversarial production of fabricated citations at length and then publishes an unmetered public oracle over the detection signal, with no statement on misuse, gives a reader no sign the question was asked.

**E5 — Potential design or analysis problem: the export path invites wholesale, invisible substitution of the user's citations.**

- **Location**: Section 4 para 3 (p7); Algorithm 1 lines 6 and 32 (p4); Figure 1 (p5).
- **Observation**: The paper urges users to use the batch export to "replace potentially erroneous original entries", and asserts that outputs derive from authoritative metadata, "ensuring correctly formatted citations with validated bibliographic details". The word "ensuring" invites trust without inspection, so the tool's failure mode is the same corruption of the scholarly record it is built to prevent.
