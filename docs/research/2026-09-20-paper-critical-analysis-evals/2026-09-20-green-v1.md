## 2. Summary

- **Problem.** LLMs fabricate plausible references; reference managers catalogue whatever they are given; database checking by hand is slow; commercial detectors are rate-limited or paid. The objective is a free, open, web-based tool that verifies one reference or a whole BibTeX file against several scholarly databases in seconds and returns corrected APA and BibTeX (abstract; §1 ¶1, ¶4–5). No hypotheses; the use cases are enumerated in §5 ¶1 (authors pre-submission, LLM-assisted literature search, reviewers, publisher pipelines, bibliometric data cleaning).

- **Method.** None of the five families in `references/method-menu.md` (experimental, correlational, survey, archival, qualitative) applies; this is a **systems / tool-description paper**, so the calibration is architecture decisions, scalability evidence, real-world deployment, and engineering contribution. That choice means the results can show what the system is designed to do, not how well it does it. There are no participants, materials, procedure, variables, pilot, or sample; those slots are not applicable.
  The design (§3; Fig. 1; Alg. 1; Eqs. 1–3): React/TypeScript web app with four modules (LaTeX-command filter, BibTeX parser, multi-source search, presentation/export). Input is cleaned of LaTeX commands, parsed as BibTeX or free text, and turned into a query. CrossRef is queried for three candidates; if none, Semantic Scholar. Candidates are scored on normalized Levenshtein title similarity (Eq. 1: 1 − lev/max length, after lowercasing and stripping non-alphanumerics), author family-name containment ("proportion of matched authors"), journal and year match. If the score is below 70 or issues are detected, Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex are queried, author lists are intersected across the three sources, a +10 bonus is added when at least two authors are confirmed, metadata is merged, and non-intersecting authors are flagged as "Potential fabricated authors." Confidence is Eq. 2 (title > 80, author < 90: S_title − 0.5·(100 − S_author)) or Eq. 3 (average of title, author, journal, year similarities plus a bonus in [0, 10]), with penalties of −20 for title or author mismatch, −10 to −20 for journal discrepancy and per fake author. Existence is `bestScore > 50` (Alg. 1 l.32); Fig. 1 labels results Verified (> 80) or Partial Match. Outputs are APA 7th and BibTeX generated from database metadata. A footnote states the thresholds were "empirically calibrated" (fn. 2).
  Features (§4): a single input box with quick-check and batch-check modes; batch runs sequentially at 800 ms intervals with progressive status; download or copy of corrected BibTeX. §4.1 gives Table 1, a 13-row feature matrix against Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote, JabRef, on which CheckIfExist alone has the six validation-related rows.

- **Results.** None. The paper reports no data, no descriptive or inferential statistics, no tables of outcomes, no example runs (N1). `references/quantitative-results.md` therefore has nothing to apply. What stands in for results is the architecture description, Alg. 1, Eqs. 1–3, and Table 1, all of which are design statements or self-assessments.

- **Discussion.** There is no discussion section; §5 "Conclusions" lists use cases and restates the contribution. Why the authors think the design works: the coverage argument (no single database is complete; §3 ¶2) and the diagnostic argument (fabricated authors are a strong hallucination signal; fn. 2, p.6). Practical value: pre-submission checks, reviewer audits, publisher pipelines, bibliometric cleaning, and re-import of corrected BibTeX into Zotero (§4.1 ¶2; §5 ¶1). Limitations stated: only the generic coverage caveats in §3 ¶2 (CrossRef may lack preprints, regional journals, older works; OpenAlex metadata quality varies). No limitations section; no false-positive or false-negative discussion. Future research: none. ## 3. Discussion

### Importance

**Score: 3/5.**

The problem is important and timely, and the paper argues that convincingly: citations underpin the scholarly record and its metrics (§1 ¶2), LLMs fabricate references at rates the paper puts at 6% to over 30% (§2 ¶2, citing Agrawal et al. 2024), and fabricated references have reached accepted NeurIPS papers (§1 ¶3, via Goldman 2026). The economic framing in §2 ¶3 (generation cost near zero, verification cost borne by readers) is a clear statement of why automation is needed.

The contribution is a free web tool with a specific pipeline. The big ideas are (i) cascade across three metadata sources, (ii) cross-source author agreement as a hallucination signal, and (iii) return corrected BibTeX rather than a verdict alone. The question ("can references be verified quickly and freely?") matches the contribution (a tool); no mismatch there. But significance is unmeasured: without any evaluation the reader cannot tell whether the tool catches the hallucinations the introduction describes — particularly the "subtle alterations of real papers" the paper itself identifies (§1 ¶3), which a title-similarity matcher would likely resolve to the real paper and pass (N16).

- **Location:** §1 ¶3 vs §3 p.6. **Observation:** The introduction defines hallucinations to include subtle alterations of real papers; the method matches on title similarity and corrects from database metadata. **Evidence or criterion:** N16 — no statement of whether alterations are flagged or silently corrected. **Why it matters:** The class of hallucination most likely to survive peer review is the one the method appears least suited to surface.

Evidence: §1 ¶2–3; §3 ¶2, p.6; §2 ¶2–3; §5 ¶1; N1; N16.

### Novelty

**Score: 2/5.**

Nothing in the method is new as an algorithm: normalized Levenshtein similarity (Eq. 1), family-name containment, threshold-and-penalty scoring, and lookups against CrossRef, Semantic Scholar, and OpenAlex are all standard components. What is claimed as new is the combination and the packaging — cascade across three sources, cross-source author intersection, LaTeX cleaning, batch BibTeX, corrected BibTeX output, unlimited free use. The clever element is treating cross-source author agreement as a hallucination signal, but its independence assumption is weakened by the paper's own description of OpenAlex (Credibility). Is the approach obvious? Largely: querying authoritative metadata APIs is the natural response to the problem, as §2 ¶3 itself argues ("automated validation systems capable of querying authoritative metadata sources"). New information to be learned: the specific score formulas and penalties, though not their justification (N2).

The "fills this gap" claim (abstract; §1 ¶5; §4.1) is broader than the cited literature supports: no prior verification tool, academic or commercial, is named or compared (N5, N6), and the one citation attached to the commercial-services sentence is by title a paper about LLM risks in peer review. Whether comparable tools exist is not something this report can assert without web access; what it can say is that the paper does not establish that they do not. Incremental engineering, plausibly useful; not a different kind of work.

Evidence: abstract; §1 ¶4–5; §2 ¶3; §3 ¶2, p.6; §4.1 last ¶; Eq. 1; N2, N5, N6.

### Applicability

**Score: 3/5.**

Practical applications are concrete and the paper states them clearly (§5 ¶1): pre-submission bibliography checks, verifying LLM-suggested references, reviewer audits, publisher pipelines, bibliometric data cleaning; plus the Zotero export → validate → re-import loop (§4.1 ¶2). The tool is free, browser-based, MIT-licensed, and accepts free-text or BibTeX, so any researcher can try it today at no cost — that is real applicability. The corrected-BibTeX output is the feature most likely to be adopted.

Applying it to one's own work requires knowing the error rates, which are not reported (N1), so the sensible use is as a first-pass screen whose "Potential fabricated authors" and "Partial Match" labels are treated as prompts for manual checking, not verdicts (N18). The publisher-pipeline use case is not supported by anything described: the paper presents a web page with a text box, no API, no server component (N7, N17). For other researchers and practitioners: the design is simple enough to reimplement from §3, except that the scoring rules are incompletely specified (N9, N10, N12).

Evidence: §4 ¶1–3; §4.1 ¶2; §5 ¶1; Availability; N1, N7, N9, N10, N12, N17, N18.

### Generalizability

**Score: 2/5.**

Not a sampling question — there is no sample — but a coverage question. The results, such as they are, are claims about a system whose behaviour depends entirely on what the three databases index and how their author strings look. The paper concedes that CrossRef "may lack coverage of preprints, regional journals, or older publications" and that OpenAlex "metadata quality varies" (§3 ¶2), then makes no provision for it (N15). Books, chapters, theses, reports, and grey literature — common in the humanities and social sciences — are never mentioned. The fake-author heuristic depends on English capitalization conventions (capitalized tokens = candidate surnames, p.6) and the normalization strips non-alphanumerics (Eq. 1 context), so non-Latin scripts, diacritics, and particles are outside its design envelope (N13, N14). Tight internal control was not bought at the cost of external validity; rather, no evidence of behaviour under any condition is given, so the reader cannot tell how far the design extends beyond English-language, DOI-registered journal and conference literature. Subgroup consistency: not assessable.

Evidence: §3 ¶2; §3 p.6 (author matching; normalization); Eq. 1; N13, N14, N15.

### Scalability

**Score: 2/5.**

Computational cost is trivial (Levenshtein over titles); the paper does not discuss it, and it is not the constraint. The constraint is third-party API throughput, and the one number the paper gives sets it: batch mode is sequential at 800 ms intervals (§4, p.7). That is a lower bound of 80 s for a 100-entry bibliography and 4 min for 300 entries, before API latency, and before the fallback's two additional queries per uncertain reference (Alg. 1 l.10–11). The abstract's "within seconds" is a single-reference statement (C11). Downward, the tool scales fine — one reference is its natural unit. Upward, the §5 use cases "publishers can integrate reference validation into submission pipelines" and "large-scale bibliometric analyses" are not supported by anything described: no API, no server, no batching beyond a text box, no statement of API quotas or authentication (N7, N8, N17). Inference, not a paper statement: a GitHub Pages URL implies static hosting, which would mean all API calls originate from the user's browser and are subject to per-client rate limits.

Evidence: abstract; §4 p.7 ("800ms intervals"); Alg. 1 l.10–11; §5 ¶1; Availability; C11; N7, N8, N17.

### Assumptions

**Score: 2/5.**

Key assumptions, mostly implicit:

1. *Presence in a database = authenticity; absence = fabrication.* Stated as the design premise (§1 ¶5; §3 ¶2). Realistic for DOI-registered journal literature; unrealistic for books, grey literature, and regional or non-English venues, as the paper half-admits (§3 ¶2; N15). Absent references produce fabricated-author warnings under Alg. 1 l.18–19 (C5).
2. *Three sources are independent witnesses.* Contradicted by the paper's own statement that OpenAlex aggregates CrossRef (§3 ¶2). Not acknowledged.
3. *Title similarity above a threshold identifies the intended work.* The variable "hallucinated reference" is defined broadly in §1 ¶3 to include altered real papers; the operational definition is "no candidate scores above 50/70/80," which does not cover that case (N16). The definitions of the score thresholds themselves are asserted as calibrated (fn. 2) with nothing shown (N2).
4. *Capitalized tokens not matching known fields are candidate fake authors* (p.6). Realistic only for English-style references without capitalized venue words (N14).
5. *Author similarity below 90 with title above 80 warrants Eq. 2; "high matching across all fields" warrants Eq. 3.* The second condition is undefined and the remaining cases are unspecified (N10).

No parametric test is used, so no distributional assumption to check; no within-subjects design, so no transfer assumption. Scope is partly explicit (three named sources; what the tool does not do, §4.1 ¶2) but the assumptions above are not stated as assumptions anywhere.

Evidence: §1 ¶3, ¶5; §3 ¶2, p.6; fn. 2; Alg. 1 l.18–19; Eqs. 2–3; C5; N2, N10, N14, N15, N16.

### Ethics

**Score: 3/5.**

IRB approval, informed consent, deception, debriefing, and participant treatment do not apply: there are no human subjects. What applies:

- *Is the work a good idea?* Yes: a free validator lowers the cost of checking, which the paper correctly identifies as the asymmetry driving the problem (§2 ¶3).
- *Potential harms, and whether the authors are aware of them.* Not discussed. The tool labels people "Potential fabricated authors" (Alg. 1 l.27) on the basis of an intersection that, as written, empties when one index lacks the record (C5), and it emits "corrected" BibTeX that replaces the user's entry with a matched record (§4 ¶3) — if the match is wrong, the tool has manufactured a citation error under a "validated" label. A false "fabricated" flag in a reviewer's or publisher's hands (§5 ¶1) is a reputational harm to an author. Coverage bias against books, non-English, and non-DOI work (N15) means those literatures are more likely to be flagged. None of these is acknowledged; there is no limitations section.
- *Privacy.* Whether pasted references — which can reveal an unpublished manuscript's bibliography — are transmitted to third-party APIs or logged is not stated (N20).
- *Integrity.* No funding, conflict-of-interest, or contribution statement (N19). The code is stated to be open under MIT with a URL (Availability), which is the right transparency posture; no data are shared because none were collected. No observable sign of fabricated data (there are no data), plagiarism, undeserved authorship (sole author), or duplicate publication in the text. The reference-list oddities in C10 are consistent with early-view dating and are not an integrity signal.

Evidence: §2 ¶3; §4 ¶3; §4.1 last ¶; §5 ¶1; Alg. 1 l.27; Availability; C5, C10; N15, N19, N20.
