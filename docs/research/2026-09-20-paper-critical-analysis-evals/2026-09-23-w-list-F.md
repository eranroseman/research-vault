### 2. Summary

- **Problem**: No research question and no hypothesis is stated. The objective (Section 1, paragraph 1) is to introduce "CheckIfExist, an open-source tool for real-time validation of bibliographic references against multiple scholarly databases, designed to address the growing problem of citation hallucinations in AI-generated academic content." The problem is framed as a gap in the toolchain: reference managers "are fundamentally designed for organization rather than validation" (Section 1, paragraph 4); database search requires "manual querying of each citation—a process that scales poorly and interrupts the writing workflow" (Section 1, paragraph 4); and commercial detection services restrict free use (Section 1, paragraph 4). No objective is stated in measurable terms — there is no target accuracy, latency, coverage, or adoption figure anywhere in the paper.
- **Method**: No family from the Method menu fits: the paper runs no experiment, no correlational or archival analysis, no survey, and no qualitative study. The Paper Type recorded at stage 1 — **systems**, the nearest of the seven, with the mismatch named there — stands in its place. The paper's evaluation has no family either, because the paper contains no evaluation: no dataset, no labelled reference set, no baseline, no measurement (N1). What is reported instead is a design: a React/TypeScript web application in four modules (Section 3, paragraph 1); a cascading query order CrossRef → Semantic Scholar → OpenAlex (Section 3, paragraph 2; Algorithm 1, lines 2-11); normalised Levenshtein title similarity (Eq. 1); family-name containment author matching with a fabricated-author heuristic (Section 3, paragraph 7 (p. 6)); a composite confidence score in two branches (Eqs. 2 and 3) with additive penalties (Section 3, paragraph 8 (p. 6); footnote 2); an 800 ms sequential batch interval (Section 4, paragraph 2 (p. 7)); APA 7th-edition and BibTeX output generated from retrieved metadata (Section 4, paragraph 3 (p. 7)). What that choice implies for what the results can and cannot show: the paper can show that a design exists and what it is intended to do; it cannot show that the design works, how often it is right, or how it fails.
- **Results**: None are reported. There is no data analysis, no descriptive statistic, no table or chart of measurements, and no inferential statistic (N1, N3, N14). The two non-prose artifacts are descriptive rather than results: Figure 1 is a flowchart of the intended pipeline, and Table 1 is a thirteen-row feature-presence grid across five tools with no source, version, or access date for any cell (N16). The only numbers the paper supplies about its own system are design parameters: score thresholds of 70 (Algorithm 1, line 8; Figure 1), 80 (Eq. 2's stated condition; Figure 1) and 50 (Algorithm 1, line 32); penalties of -20 for title and author mismatches and -10 to -20 for journal discrepancies and each detected fake author (Section 3, paragraph 8 (p. 6)); a multi-source bonus of up to 10 (Eq. 3; Algorithm 1, line 22); three CrossRef candidates retrieved per query (Algorithm 1, line 2; Figure 1); and the 800 ms batch interval. Footnote 2 states these were "empirically calibrated to optimize discrimination between valid references and known hallucinations" and reports nothing whatever about that calibration (N2).
- **Discussion (the authors' own)**: The paper has no discussion section; Section 5, "Conclusions", carries what discussion there is, in two paragraphs. Interpretation and implications: five use cases are proposed — authors validating reference lists before submission, researchers checking LLM-suggested citations, reviewers auditing manuscripts under review, publishers integrating validation into submission pipelines, and bibliometricians filtering spurious references from citation-network datasets (Section 5, paragraph 1). Practical value: the tool "enables researchers to validate citations at the speed of modern content production", complements rather than replaces reference managers, and is free and open (Section 5, paragraph 2). How the work advances knowledge: the paper claims a filled gap in the scholarly toolkit rather than new knowledge, which is consistent with a tool paper. Why the authors think they got their results: the question does not arise — no results are reported. Limitations: none are stated anywhere in the paper (N17), including the false-positive problem, database coverage, and dependence on third-party APIs, even though Section 3, paragraph 2 names each database's coverage gaps when arguing for the cascade. Future research and what the author plans next: not stated (N18). The conclusion reiterates the problem's significance and the tool's contribution, summarises no methods and no results — there are none to summarise — and introduces the five use cases for the first time (C17). There is no acknowledgements section, no funding statement and no conflict-of-interest statement (N20); the paper closes with a one-paragraph "Availability" section giving the MIT licence and the tool URL.

### 3. Discussion

#### Importance

The problem is real and timely, but the paper's contribution is an unevaluated tool whose output measures database absence rather than the hallucination the title promises.

- **Observation**: The title and abstract promise detection of citation hallucinations, and Algorithm 1 operationalises that as a database match score above 50, while the paper's own Section 1 describes hallucinations that are "subtle alterations of real papers" and would match the real record with high title similarity.

- **Evidence or criterion**: Title; Section 1, paragraph 3; Algorithm 1, line 32; N28; C11; Claim 1.

- **Why it matters**: A reader is told the tool detects hallucination when it detects non-match, so a chimeric or lightly altered fabrication can be returned as existing and an unindexed real work as non-existent.

- **Kind**: Not reported.
