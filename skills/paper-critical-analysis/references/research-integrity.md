# Background: research integrity

## Four things that are not the same

Do not infer misconduct, poor quality, or merit from a missing reporting item. Separate:

- **Not reported:** the paper does not provide enough information to assess the point.
- **Potential design or analysis problem:** the reported method may not answer the stated question.
- **Demonstrated inconsistency:** two locations in the paper conflict.
- **Integrity concern:** credible evidence, described neutrally.

## Questions the Discussion asks

Any sign of research-integrity concerns: fabricated or adjusted data, plagiarism, undeserved authorship, duplicate publication without acknowledgement, or refusal to share data on request?

## Ethics and integrity: what to check, and how to word it

Check applicable approvals, consent, welfare, privacy, community governance, funding, sponsor role, conflicts, authorship/contribution, registration, biosafety, and dual-use concerns.

The reader identifies concerns and does not adjudicate misconduct, accuse authors, or investigate them. Record the exact location and observable discrepancy. Describe uncertainty and plausible benign explanations.

## Fabrication and other misconduct

Safeguards: publish raw data, or at least keep good records of it.

Other misconduct, per the APA: not making mistakes public once found; plagiarism; publication credit for work not done; duplicate publication without acknowledgement; refusing to share raw data with other scientists; falsifying credentials.

## Conflicts of interest

Conflicts of interest: funding source and commercial interests disclosed; author-participant relationships disclosed; industry sponsorship terms (data access, publication rights) reviewed.

## Questionable research practices — watch for

- In experimental design: manipulating confounding variables or demand characteristics to get the result you want
- In collecting data: biased coding — use more than one coder and check their agreement
- In data analysis: using statistical tests without checking their assumptions; eliminating data from participants who don't fit the hypothesis, without good, clearly reported reasons (excluding an extreme outlier trial is usually easier to justify than excluding a whole participant)
- In reporting results: chart tricks (showing only part of the axis; distorting axes; using 2D for a 1D variable); reporting only the results that support the hypothesis; hiding a poorly designed experiment, or every analysis that was tried; reordering experiments into a logical rather than chronological sequence without saying so; shifting emphasis to a more interesting question than the one originally asked without saying so
