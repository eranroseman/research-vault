### Background: types of sources, and how current they are

The ranking applies to the paper's own venue and to what it cites.

- Books: cover the field up to roughly 13 years before publication; good for foundations; reviewed less rigorously than journals; each author biased toward one approach.
- Review articles and edited-book chapters: narrower and more current, within 5-8 years; not always reviewed as rigorously as journal articles.
- Journal articles: the primary sources, giving the results as interpreted by those who did the work; the most current formal source, appearing a few years after the work began; top journals accept 10-20% of submissions.
- Proceedings: peer-reviewed, but shorter and less rigorously; timely; common in applied fields.
- Technical reports: more procedural detail; usually not peer-reviewed.
- Preprints and web sources: no mandatory quality control; check the author's credentials and corroborate.

### Background: the research-method menu

The family sets what to expect from the method and results sections, and which weaknesses to watch for.

- **Experiment** (quantitative): manipulates an independent variable and observes a dependent one.
- **Correlational observation** (quantitative): relates two variables and manipulates neither. It cannot show causality: the direction is unknown, and a third variable may drive both. A causal conclusion needs the other variables ruled out.
- **Survey** (quantitative): asks people what they do or think. It reveals motivation and gathers volume fast, but people lie or say what the researcher wants, and self-report replaces observation.
- **Archival research** (quantitative): analyzes existing records (census data, videos, accident or crime reports, voting records, publications). The data is extensive, but it yields relationships rather than causes, and the records may be unreliable. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews (PRISMA) are archival research over publications.
- **Qualitative designs**: used when it is too early to know the questions; inductive studies, ethnographies, naturalistic observation, case histories.

The two combine: qualitative methods develop hypotheses, quantitative methods test them.

### Background: designing an experiment

**Variables.** Independent: manipulated by the researcher, with several values. Dependent: measures the participants' behavior. Control: held constant; aids replication, but over-control hurts external validity (a quiet, well-lit lab may not mirror real use). Random: varied at random, possibly within constraints, as in random assignment against bias. Confounding: changes along with the independent variable.

**Defining variables.** A sound paper defines its variables explicitly enough to replicate; some (visual complexity, stress) resist definition. Its independent-variable range is realistic enough to show an effect; a pilot finds that range. Its dependent variable states what is measured and how, with its reliability (the same result again, and the expected error) and its validity (it measures the intended thing).

**Threats to internal validity.** Selection: non-random assignment, self-selection, early versus late volunteers. Mortality: dropouts in long studies. Testing: a pretest changes posttest answers or reveals the hypothesis. Statistical regression: extreme scorers carry more measurement error and drift toward the mean on retest.

**Between- versus within-subjects.** Between: each participant sees one value; no carry-over or learning, less fatigue, and sometimes the only option (age); but it needs more participants and equivalent groups (random assignment). Within: each participant sees all values; fewer participants, less individual variance, and visible preferences; but exposure itself changes behavior (learning, fatigue, maturation). Order effects are handled by counterbalancing (half do A then B, half B then A, which assumes symmetrical transfer) or, with many values, by a Latin square (each value appears in, before, and after every position equally often; the participant count is a multiple of the value count). Matched groups: a pretest on measures expected to affect behavior; the study runs longer, and the pretest may itself affect performance.

**Number of variables.** Single, two-level (experimental versus control): easy to interpret, blind to linearity. Multi-level: shows the relationship's shape at the cost of more time, participants, and analysis. Factorial: crosses factors; reveals interactions and gives more precise, more generalizable results; mixed when some factors are within- and some between-subjects; ANOVA assumes continuous, normally distributed data; harder to interpret once interactions appear. Converging series: several experiments on one question, each fixing a level for the next or eliminating hypotheses; flexible, but interactions across experiments are hard to assess, and there is no random assignment between experiments.

### Background: interpreting quantitative results

- Histograms show whether the data looks normal enough for the test used.
- Charts: independent variable on x, dependent on y; bars for categorical, lines for numeric; error bars usually two standard errors, sometimes one standard deviation. Truncated or distorted axes exaggerate differences.
- Scatterplots show whether two variables are related; a correlation coefficient gives the strength.
- Factorial designs have main effects (each variable alone) and interactions (one variable's effect depends on another's level); crossover interactions may show no main effect.
- Parametric tests (t-test, ANOVA) assume a normal population, and some also assume sphericity; non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis) serve when normality is absent or unknown.
- A result is called statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05 by convention; a sound paper reports all p values, since one just above 0.05 may warrant follow-up.
- Two misreadings: a non-significant difference does not mean the values are equal, and "significant" without "statistically" conflates statistical with practical significance.

### Background: qualitative methods

**Collection and analysis.** Participants or sites are selected purposefully. Data comes from observations, interviews, documents, and audiovisual materials. The data is organized, read through for a general sense, then coded (with expected or surprising codes, possibly tied to a theoretical perspective) into descriptions, themes, or categories, often hierarchical; aggregate dimensions follow, and from their relations, a grounded model.

**Validation.** Multiple coders. Triangulation across sources. Rich, thick description (or diagrams and counts). The researcher's own bias disclosed.

**Strategies.** Grounded theory: a theory of a process grounded in participants' views, built by open coding, concurrent collection and analysis, constant comparison, saturation, and axial and selective coding. Case study: a program, event, activity, process, or individuals, chosen as typical, diverse, or extreme cases. Ethnography: a cultural group in its setting over time. Phenomenology: the essence of an experience. Narrative: lives retold as chronology. Participatory research: participants set priorities, co-produce artifacts, and learn the results.

### Background: treating participants fairly, and research integrity

**Misconduct.** Fabrication: data made up or adjusted, images doctored. Errors found but not published. Plagiarism. Authorship without contribution. Duplicate publication without acknowledgement. Raw data refused on request. Falsified credentials. Safeguards: published raw data or good records, and multiple investigators, who notice problems.

**Questionable research practices.** Design: confounds or demand characteristics steered toward the wanted result (guards: double-blind runs, or a neutral observer). Collection: biased coding (guard: several coders, with agreement checked). Analysis: tests used without their assumptions checked; participants dropped for not fitting the hypothesis without clear, reported reasons (dropping an outlier trial is easier to justify than dropping a whole participant). Reporting: chart tricks (partial or distorted axes, 2D for a 1D variable); only the supporting results; a poorly designed experiment or the analyses tried kept hidden (focusing on the informative analyses is fine); experiments reordered without saying so; the question shifted to a more interesting one without saying so.

**Fair treatment.** The researcher holds power, participants may feel evaluated, and fairness is the researcher's responsibility. Signs: sessions on time and prepared; data kept confidential and never released identifiably; interactions limited to the study; participants made comfortable.

**IRB.** Approval is required before human-subjects research begins; the board's concerns are informed consent, confidentiality, and minimized physical and psychological risk.

**Informed consent.** Before the study: what participants will do, the nature of the research, consequences of declining or withdrawing, risks, time, discomfort, adverse effects. Plain language, no pressure, acknowledged (typically signed). Children: a parent decides, the child may still opt out.

**Demand characteristics.** Participants' expectations, brought along or picked up from the consent form or the researcher; they may try to confirm the hypothesis, or more often manage how they are seen. Guard: one standard script, tone included.

**Deception.** Hiding the study's real purpose; the trend is toward withholding rather than misleading. Judge it by whether it was necessary to answer the question, whether participants were bothered, and whether they were debriefed.
