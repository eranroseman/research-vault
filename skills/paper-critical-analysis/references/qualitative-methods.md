# Background: qualitative methods

## What the Summary reports

How participants or sites were purposefully selected; what data was collected; the coding process; what it produced.

## What sound qualitative work looks like

1. Codes are interpretive.
2. Stay close to the data: participants' language in early codes (in vivo) before abstracting.
3. Document every analytic move.
4. Don't out-source interpretation to NLP.

## Sampling

- **Saturation** is the goal. Typical: 6-12 interviews for narrow scope, 20-30 for grounded theory.
- **Purposive** is the default.

## Case selection and participatory research

Case study — selecting typical, diverse, or extreme cases. How far do the authors claim the resulting themes or model extend? Participatory research gives participants a crucial role: helping set research priorities, collaborating on research artifacts, and learning about the results.

## Trustworthiness and reflexivity

Trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba):

- **Credibility** — triangulation, member checking, prolonged engagement.
- **Transferability** — thick description.
- **Dependability** — audit trail of decisions.
- **Confirmability** — reflexivity statement on the authors' positionality.

In a positionality statement — who the authors are, their relationship to the topic and participants, what biases they bring.

## Analytic traditions, and what each should report

Reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke): familiarization; initial coding across the corpus; theme construction; theme review against coded data and the full corpus; theme defining and naming (a one-sentence essence for each); an interpretive narrative with extracts as evidence.

Grounded theory (Charmaz / Strauss-Corbin): open coding, line-by-line; axial coding into categories; selective coding to the core category; theoretical sampling; constant comparison; continue until theoretical saturation.

Framework analysis (Ritchie & Spencer): a thematic framework from familiarization; every transcript indexed against it; data charted into a matrix (rows = participants, columns = themes). Best when there are pre-defined topics.

Content analysis (Krippendorff / Hsieh & Shannon): conventional (codes emerge from data), directed (theory-derived codes, refined), or summative (count occurrences, then interpret context). Best when frequency matters and reliability is critical.

IPA: idiographic, one transcript fully before the next; descriptive → linguistic → conceptual comments; superordinate themes within case, then patterns across cases.

## Inter-rater reliability and the self-audit a paper should pass

With two or more coders: both code a subset (typically 20-30% of transcripts); Cohen's κ for two coders, Fleiss' κ for 3+, or Krippendorff's α. Targets: κ ≥ 0.80 strong, 0.60-0.79 substantial, 0.40-0.59 moderate.

Does the paper show:

- Codebook consistency — every code has a clear definition and discriminating examples.
- Themes with a clear central organizing concept (not just topic buckets).
- Each theme supported by quotes from multiple participants (or the limit acknowledged).
- Disconfirming cases addressed.
- Reflexivity statement included.
- An audit trail reconstructable from memos and codebook versions.
- If NLP or LLM-assisted coding was used, validation against hand-coding documented.
